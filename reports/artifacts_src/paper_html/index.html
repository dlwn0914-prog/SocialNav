Learning Individual Walking-Style Preferences for Crowd Navigation
from a Few Pairwise Queries

Anonymous

Abstract

People differ in how they want a robot to move through a crowd: some prefer it to yield early, others to keep pace; some want it to pass behind them, others in front. Existing crowd-navigation policies optimize a fixed notion of social compliance and cannot adapt to such individual preferences. We present a framework that identifies a user’s walking-style preference from a handful of pairwise (A/B) queries and uses it to select the robot’s path at test time. A VQ-VAE trained on human pedestrian trajectories provides a codebook of walking behaviours that generates style-diverse, human-like candidate paths. Unsafe candidates are filtered out, and each remaining candidate is described by interpretable style features: passing side, passing in front or behind, yielding, detour width, and human-likeness. A Bayesian Bradley–Terry model over these features is updated after each answer, and queries are chosen by the expected utility of the best option (EUBO). On held-out situations from Grand Central and ETH/UCY with simulated users, five queries reduce the normalized simple regret from 0.45–0.47 (human-data prior) to 0.09–0.12, outperforming random queries in all four settings and a DPO-trained estimator on the same budget and matching an information-gain query; the advantage over random queries persists when simulated users answer noisily or have nonlinear preferences. On shared Grand Central scenes, codebook candidates leave 0.11 regret after five queries against 0.35 for candidates from a flow-matching crowd-navigation generator, whose narrower candidate set bounds what any preference can achieve. In a closed-loop replay, following the learned preference keeps the user’s step regret low without raising the collision rate.

1 Introduction

Robots increasingly share sidewalks, stations and shopping streets with people, and how such a robot moves through a crowd matters to the person it serves – the user who sends a delivery robot, rides in a wheelchair or walks alongside a guide robot. “Socially compliant” navigation, however, is not a single behaviour. Facing an oncoming pedestrian, one user wants the robot to slow down and let them pass, another wants it to keep pace; one prefers it to pass behind people, another in front; one tolerates a wide detour around a group, another wants the shortest safe path. People themselves differ in how close they pass others and how readily they give way (Hall 1966), so a single notion of good crowd behaviour leaves many users dissatisfied.

Crowd-navigation methods nonetheless optimize one fixed objective. Model-based planners encode hand-tuned social costs, reinforcement-learning policies maximize a designed reward, and imitation or generative planners reproduce the average pedestrian of their training data (Helbing and Molnár 1995; Berg et al. 2011; Chen et al. 2019; Kretzschmar et al. 2016; Mavrogiannis et al. 2023). Adapting any of them to an individual requires retuning or retraining. Preference-based learning offers a direct route: instead of specifying a reward, the user compares two behaviours and the robot infers what they want (Christiano et al. 2017; Sadigh et al. 2017). In robotics, however, each comparison costs the user time and attention, so a preference must be identified from a handful of answers, and the trajectories being compared must be meaningful to a person: safe, plausible, and different in ways the user cares about.

Our key idea is to separate what the robot can do from which of those behaviours a particular user wants (Fig. 1). For the first, we learn a discrete codebook of human walking behaviours with a VQ-VAE (Oord et al. 2017) trained on pedestrian trajectories. Decoding the codebook in a given situation yields a small set of candidate paths that are human-like by construction and differ in style – passing left or right, ahead or behind, slowing down or keeping pace – and a safety filter removes the ones that come too close to anyone. For the second, we describe each safe candidate by five interpretable style features and model the user’s preference as a linear utility over them, learned with a Bayesian Bradley–Terry model (Bradley and Terry 1952) from A/B answers. Because the robot only executes one path per situation after querying, the relevant objective is simple regret, and we choose each question by the expected utility of the best option (EUBO) (Astudillo et al. 2023), which is one-step Bayes-optimal for it.

The data prior alone does not capture individual preferences: on held-out situations from Grand Central and ETH/UCY, always taking the most human-like safe path leaves a normalized simple regret of 0.45–0.47 for simulated users, close to a random safe path (0.49). Five EUBO queries reduce it to 0.09–0.12, with lower regret than random questions in all four settings and than an active-query baseline in three, and as low as an information-gain query; the advantage over random questions holds for noisy, inconsistent and nonlinear simulated users. The comparison with Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al. 2023), the estimator behind much of current preference fine-tuning, shows where the gain comes from: given the same questions, DPO and the Bradley–Terry posterior perform alike, but DPO has no posterior with which to choose its questions. The candidate set matters as well: on shared Grand Central scenes, the candidates of a flow-matching crowd-navigation planner (Singha et al. 2026) cover a much narrower range of behaviours than the codebook, which bounds what any learned preference can achieve (union regret 0.35 vs 0.11 after five queries). Finally, in a closed-loop replay, following the learned preference keeps the user’s step regret at 0.10–0.14 without a significant change in collision rate.

Contributions.

  • A walking-behaviour codebook learned with a VQ-VAE from pedestrian trajectories, which turns a situation into a small set of diverse, human-like candidate paths that a safety filter can check (Sec. 3.2).

  • A linear preference over five interpretable style features, learned with a Bayesian Bradley–Terry model from A/B answers, with questions chosen by EUBO to minimize simple regret (Sec. 3.4).

  • A simple-regret evaluation protocol on held-out situations (Sec. 3.5) and experiments on Grand Central and ETH/UCY that compare query selection (including information gain and users outside the model), a DPO estimator, a flow-matching candidate generator, and closed-loop execution (Sec. 4).

2 Related Work

Crowd navigation. Robot navigation among people has been surveyed from the human-aware (Kruse et al. 2013) and the social-navigation perspective (Mavrogiannis et al. 2023). Model-based methods treat pedestrians as particles under attractive and repulsive forces (Helbing and Molnár 1995) or as agents that share the responsibility for avoiding each other (Berg et al. 2011), and interaction-aware planners predict the crowd and the robot jointly to avoid the freezing-robot problem (Trautman and Krause 2010). Learning-based methods train collision-avoidance policies with deep reinforcement learning (Chen, Liu, et al. 2017), add social norms such as passing on one side to the reward (Chen, Everett, et al. 2017), model crowd–robot interaction with attention (Chen et al. 2019), or recover a navigation cost from human demonstrations with inverse reinforcement learning (Kretzschmar et al. 2016). Pedestrian trajectory prediction (Alahi et al. 2016; Gupta et al. 2018) and generative planners based on diffusion (Janner et al. 2022) or flow matching (Lipman et al. 2023) provide multimodal samples of plausible motion; Crowd-FM (Singha et al. 2026), the crowd-navigation planner we compare against as a candidate generator, draws such samples with conditional flow matching, refines them with a projection-based optimizer (Rastgar et al. 2024) and selects one with a score learned from human demonstrations. All of these encode one notion of social compliance, fixed by hand-tuned costs, a designed reward or the average of the training data; a different user’s preference requires retuning or retraining. We keep the generative part, a codebook learned from pedestrian data, but leave the choice among its safe candidates to a preference identified from the individual user.

Learning from pairwise preferences. Pairwise comparisons are commonly modelled with the Bradley–Terry model (Bradley and Terry 1952). Preference-based reward learning was scaled up with deep networks by Christiano et al. (2017) and became the basis of RLHF for language models (Ouyang et al. 2022). Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al. 2023) removes the separate reward model and fits the policy to the preference pairs directly, with a KL anchor to a reference policy; we use it as an estimator baseline. These methods learn from large pooled datasets of comparisons and fine-tune a high-capacity policy. Our setting is the opposite: a single user provides a handful of answers, and the learned preference only has to rank a small set of safe candidates in each new situation, which makes a low-dimensional Bayesian model and the choice of questions the deciding factors.

Active preference queries in robotics. Sadigh et al. (2017) select trajectory comparisons that maximize information about a linear reward. EUBO (Astudillo et al. 2023) asks the pair maximizing the expected utility of the better option and is one-step Bayes-optimal for simple regret; we adopt it because our objective is the one path executed after querying. UserAlign (Pădurean et al. 2025) personalizes a generative model at inference time from a few pairwise queries, treating the choice of a response from a fixed pool as best-arm identification in a logistic bandit; we use its query rule as the AQ baseline.

Discrete behaviour representations. Planners have long searched over a fixed set of motion primitives (Pivtoraiko et al. 2009). In motion prediction, anchor trajectories (Chai et al. 2019) and trajectory sets (Phan-Minh et al. 2020) turn forecasting into classification over a discrete set of futures, MotionLM (Seff et al. 2023) models motion as sequences of discrete tokens, and VQ-VAEs (Oord et al. 2017) have been used to learn a codebook for pedestrian trajectory forecasting (Benaglia et al. 2024). These works use the discrete set to predict what an agent will do; we use a codebook learned from pedestrians as the set of walking styles among which a user chooses how the robot should move.

3 Method

3.1 Problem setting

In a situation ss the robot knows its own state (speed, two past positions), its destination and the positions and velocities of nearby people. A user has a preference over how the robot walks, which we model as a linear utility over style features. The user answers a small number of A/B questions in a few query situations; the robot must then choose its path in new situations the user was never asked about.

3.2 Walking-behaviour codebook

A VQ-VAE (Oord et al. 2017) with K=16K=16 codes is trained on pedestrian futures expressed in the heading frame as deviations from constant velocity (6 points every 0.8 s, 4.8 s). Decoding every code from the robot’s current speed gives KK candidate paths. A prior network p(code∣s)p(\text{code} \mid s), trained on the same data with the destination and a summary of nearby people as input, scores how typical each candidate is for a person in that situation. One codebook and one prior are trained per dataset, on the training split of a split by person (about 80/10/10%, no person in two splits); every evaluation situation comes from the test split. A training sample is any annotated pedestrian walking at ≥\ge0.5 m/s with six future annotations available, together with up to 12 people within 6 m. The VQ-VAE (K=16K=16 codes, 8-dimensional latent) is trained with Adam (learning rate 10−310^{-3}) for 30 epochs (batch 1024) on Grand Central and 150 epochs (batch 256) on the smaller ETH/UCY. The prior is an MLP with two hidden layers of 256 units, trained with Adam (10−310^{-3}, weight decay 10−510^{-5}) for 30 epochs on the samples whose destination is far enough ahead to matter (12 s on Grand Central, 8 s on ETH/UCY); its input is the speed, the two past positions, the destination (the track’s last position) and an 8-number summary of nearby people (nearest person ahead, smallest time to a <<1 m approach, people ahead on the left and right). Dataset sizes are in Table 1.

Safety filter. People are extrapolated at constant velocity; a candidate is safe if its minimum distance to everyone is at least c=0.5c=0.5 m and its progress towards the destination is at least ρ=0.5\rho=0.5 times the best candidate’s. The preference only chooses among safe candidates:

𝒞(s)={k:dmin(s,k)≥c,prog(s,k)≥ρmaxk′prog(s,k′)}.\begin{equation} \mathcal{C}(s) = \bigl\{ k \;:\; d_{\min}(s,k) \ge c,\; \mathrm{prog}(s,k) \ge \rho \max_{k'} \mathrm{prog}(s,k') \bigr\}. \label{eq:safe} \end{equation}(1)
Situations with fewer than four safe candidates are not used.

3.3 Style features

Each safe candidate is described by ϕ(s,k)∈ℝ5\phi(s,k) \in \mathbb{R}^5, none of which has an a-priori right sign: side (tanh\tanh of the lateral offset from the conflict person at closest approach), front (crossing ahead of or behind them), yield (mean speed relative to the current speed), detour (largest deviation from the straight line to the destination) and human (log⁡p(code∣s)\log p(\text{code}\mid s)). Features are standardized over the safe candidates of the query situations.

3.4 Preference learning and query selection

The user’s utility is u(s,k)=θ⊤ϕ(s,k)u(s,k) = \theta^\top \phi(s,k). Answers follow the Bradley–Terry model (Bradley and Terry 1952) P(i≻j∣θ)=σ(θ⊤(ϕi−ϕj))P(i \succ j \mid \theta) = \sigma(\theta^\top(\phi_i - \phi_j)) with prior θ∼𝒩(0,I)\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I); the posterior is approximated by a Laplace approximation (Newton’s method for the MAP, inverse Hessian as covariance). Each query asks the pair maximizing the expected utility of the best option (Astudillo et al. 2023),

(i,j)=arg maxi≠j𝔼θ∼p(θ∣𝒟)[max⁡{ui,uj}],\begin{equation} (i,j) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{i \ne j} \; \mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})}\bigl[\max\{u_i, u_j\}\bigr], \end{equation}(2)
estimated with 512 posterior samples. At test time, in any situation, the robot executes
k̂n(s)=arg maxk∈𝒞(s)θ̂n⊤ϕ(s,k),\begin{equation} \hat k_n(s) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{k \in \mathcal{C}(s)} \hat\theta_n^{\top}\phi(s,k), \label{eq:exec} \end{equation}(3)
with θ̂n\hat\theta_n the posterior mean after nn answers.

3.5 Evaluation: simple regret

Users. For evaluation a user is a unit vector θ*\theta^\ast (‖θ*‖=1\lVert\theta^\ast\rVert = 1) with utility u*(s,k)=θ*⊤ϕ(s,k)u^\ast(s,k) = \theta^{\ast\top}\phi(s,k), who answers a question (i,j)(i,j) with P(i≻j)=σ(β(u*(s,i)−u*(s,j)))P(i \succ j) = \sigma\bigl(\beta\,(u^\ast(s,i) - u^\ast(s,j))\bigr), β=5\beta = 5. After nn answers a method returns θ̂n\hat\theta_n (the Laplace posterior mean for Bradley–Terry methods, the DPO solution for DPO, Sec. 4.3) and executes k̂n(s)\hat k_n(s) of Eq. (3) among the safe candidates 𝒞(s)\mathcal{C}(s) of Eq. (1).

Simple regret. The regret of that choice is the utility lost against the user’s best safe candidate, normalized by the utility range of the situation so that situations are comparable:

rn(s)=maxk∈𝒞(s)u*(s,k)−u*(s,k̂n(s))maxk∈𝒞(s)u*(s,k)−mink∈𝒞(s)u*(s,k)∈[0,1].\begin{equation} r_n(s) = \frac{\max_{k \in \mathcal{C}(s)} u^\ast(s,k) \;-\; u^\ast\bigl(s,\hat k_n(s)\bigr)} {\max_{k \in \mathcal{C}(s)} u^\ast(s,k) \;-\; \min_{k \in \mathcal{C}(s)} u^\ast(s,k)} \;\in [0,1]. \label{eq:regret} \end{equation}(4)
The reported number is its expectation over users and held-out test situations,
SRn=𝔼θ*[1|𝒮test|∑s∈𝒮testrn(s)],\begin{equation} \mathrm{SR}_n = \mathbb{E}_{\theta^\ast}\Bigl[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\text{test}}|} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{test}}} r_n(s)\Bigr], \label{eq:sr} \end{equation}(5)
where answers are only collected in query situations disjoint from the test situations (𝒮query∩𝒮test=∅\mathcal{S}_{\text{query}} \cap \mathcal{S}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset; sizes in Sec. 4). SRn=0\mathrm{SR}_n = 0 for the oracle (θ̂=θ*\hat\theta = \theta^\ast); a uniformly random safe candidate gives SR≈0.5\mathrm{SR} \approx 0.5.

Why simple regret. Queries cost the user’s attention but no driving utility, and what matters is the path the robot executes afterwards. The objective is therefore best-arm identification (simple regret) rather than cumulative regret over the queries. EUBO (Astudillo et al. 2023) is one-step Bayes-optimal for exactly this objective under noise-free answers, which motivates its use. Fig. 3 plots SRn\mathrm{SR}_n against the number of queries n∈{0,2,5,10,20}n \in \{0,2,5,10,20\}.

Variants used elsewhere. Closed loop: at every 0.8 s re-planning step tt of a drive, Eq. (4) is evaluated on that step’s safe set 𝒞(st)\mathcal{C}(s_t) and averaged over the steps of the episode (step regret). Candidate generators: to compare two generators AA and BB on the same situation, the max and min in Eq. (4) are taken over 𝒞A(s)∪𝒞B(s)\mathcal{C}_A(s) \cup \mathcal{C}_B(s) (union regret), so a generator whose candidates miss what the user wants is penalized even when it picks its own best; the per-generator regret of Eq. (4) is not comparable across generators. Pilot: with real participants θ*\theta^\ast is unknown; the measurable proxy is the share of held-out (test-block) answers that θ̂\hat\theta predicts.

4 Experiments

We ask: (Q1) how quickly does each query method identify a user’s style preference on situations the user was never asked about? (Q2) does the estimator matter, compared with DPO? (Q3) does the candidate generator matter? (Q4) does following the learned preference in closed loop cost reach or safety?

4.1 Setup

Data. Grand Central (Zhou et al. 2012) (GC) and ETH/UCY (Pellegrini et al. 2009; Lerner et al. 2007), split by person. Test situations are held-out pedestrians with a predicted conflict (someone passes within 1 m in the next 8 s at constant velocity) and at least four safe candidates: 12,159 on GC (destination ≥\ge12 s ahead) and 590 on ETH/UCY (≥\ge8 s, because ETH/UCY tracks are short; Appendix A.3). Table 1 gives the sizes. Grand Central is annotated every 0.8 s; ETH/UCY (biwi_eth, biwi_hotel, students001, students003, crowds_zara01, crowds_zara02) every 0.4 s and is subsampled to 0.8 s so that both codebooks see the same input.

Baselines. Random: uniformly random pairs. AQ: a version-space active query that asks the current best against its strongest challenger (Pădurean et al. 2025). All three use the same Bradley–Terry estimator as EUBO. Choosing a question takes about 0.2 ms with EUBO and 1 ms with InfoGain for 16 candidates on one CPU core (AMD Ryzen 7 9700X), so neither delays an interactive session.

4.3 Q2: Estimator – DPO

DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023) fits a policy πθ(k∣s)∝exp⁡(θ⊤ϕ(s,k))\pi_\theta(k \mid s) \propto \exp(\theta^\top\phi(s,k)) to the answered pairs with the human prior as the reference policy. Because the human feature is the standardized log-prior, the reference lies in the same family (θref=σhumanehuman\theta_{\text{ref}} = \sigma_{\text{human}} e_{\text{human}}) and the DPO loss becomes −log⁡σ(βDPO(θ−θref)⊤(ϕw−ϕl))-\log\sigma\bigl(\beta_{\text{DPO}}(\theta - \theta_{\text{ref}})^\top(\phi_w - \phi_l)\bigr): a Bradley–Terry likelihood anchored at the prior, without a posterior. We minimize it by 1,000 steps of gradient descent from θref\theta_{\text{ref}} with βDPO=0.3\beta_{\text{DPO}}=0.3, the best of ten (βDPO,steps)(\beta_{\text{DPO}}, \text{steps}) settings on GC dense (tuned on the evaluation setting, which favours DPO). Without a posterior DPO cannot choose its questions, so it uses random pairs; DPO + EUBO queries receives the pairs EUBO picks.

With the same random questions, DPO and Bradley–Terry are close after five queries and DPO is slightly worse after twenty (agreement −-0.005 to −-0.016). EUBO has lower regret than DPO in all settings (−-0.028 to −-0.056 at five queries). Given EUBO’s questions, DPO comes within 0.011 of EUBO: most of the gain comes from choosing the questions, not from the estimator.

Users outside the model. The users above answer exactly as the estimator assumes. Table 6 (Appendix) changes the users on both datasets: noisier or more consistent answers (β=1,2,10\beta=1,2,10), 20% random answers, and true utilities with pairwise feature interactions, which no linear θ\theta represents. After five queries EUBO’s regret stays below Random, AQ and DPO (95% CI) in 11 of 12 conditions; the exception is ETH/UCY with β=10\beta=10, where all methods are within 0.013. Noisy answers raise every method’s regret (EUBO 0.18–0.21 at β=1\beta=1), and on ETH/UCY InfoGain is more robust to them than EUBO (by 0.017–0.032 at β≤2\beta \le 2 and with random answers). With interactions, every method approaches the floor set by the best linear θ\theta (oracle 0.09–0.22).

4.6 User study protocol (in progress)

The synthetic users above answer with the same Bradley–Terry model the estimator assumes. Whether real people’s walking-style preferences are linear in our features, consistent, and different from person to person is what a user study has to show. We describe the protocol of a pilot study that is in progress; it has not produced results for this submission, and no claim in this paper rests on it.

Protocol. Each participant answers 20 A/B questions in a web page (about 5 minutes). A question shows the same top-down scene twice, with the robot following one of two safe candidate paths in each (animated over 4.8 s, then held for 1.2 s and repeated); the participant picks the path they would prefer the robot to take. Left/right placement is randomized per participant and question. Of the 18 distinct questions, nine come from held-out Grand Central situations (built as in Sec. 3.2, with a stricter clearance of 0.8 m so that neither option looks like a near collision), and nine from synthetic scenes the dataset rarely contains: a queue, a conversation group, a walking party and a slow walker ahead. For these a sixth, pilot-only feature group indicates whether a path passes between members of the same group. Each question pairs two candidates that differ strongly on one feature and little on the others. The destination lies outside the view (at least 15 m ahead); only its direction is drawn.

Train/test split. The first block of ten train questions (shuffled within the block) is always shown before the second block of eight test questions and two repeat questions (two train questions with the sides swapped). The page records the position of each answer, and the analysis checks that no test answer precedes the end of the train block. The train questions were chosen greedily to maximize the information about θ\theta (log⁡det⁡(I+∑qΔxqΔxq⊤)\log\det(I + \sum_q \Delta x_q \Delta x_q^\top), Δxq\Delta x_q the feature difference of question qq); in simulation, ten such questions reach a held-out agreement of 0.876 (dense users) and 0.792 (sparse), against 0.837 and 0.780 for ten random questions from the same pool and 0.928 and 0.865 for all 42 questions of an earlier 60-question version (2000 synthetic users).

Planned measures. A personal θ̂\hat\theta will be fit on each participant’s train answers (Laplace Bradley–Terry, prior 𝒩(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0,I)) and scored by the share of their test answers it predicts. It will be compared with (i) a shared θ̂\hat\theta fit on all other participants’ train answers and (ii) the human prior (always the more typical path). We will also report the repeat consistency (same path chosen when the sides are swapped), the rate of choosing the screen-left option, and the per-feature rate of choosing the option higher on that feature. In simulation, the personal–shared difference needs about 20 participants for 80% power when people differ moderately (difference 0.13), and cannot be detected when they are nearly alike; with eight test questions the per-participant accuracy has a standard deviation of about 0.14, so we will report means over participants with 95% bootstrap intervals.

5 Limitations

Synthetic users. All quantitative results use synthetic users whose answers follow the same Bradley–Terry model the estimator assumes, which favours model-based estimators; Table 6 relaxes the answer model and the linearity, but those users are still simulated; whether real people’s walking-style preferences are linear in these features, stable, and different from each other is for a user study to show. The pilot of Sec. 4.6 is in progress and reports no results here; by our power simulation it needs about 20 participants to separate personal from shared preferences when people differ moderately, and no feasible number suffices if people are nearly alike.

Linear features. The five features are hand-designed and evaluated at one conflict person; group-related preferences (cutting through a queue or a conversation) need further features.

Closed loop. Pedestrians are replayed and do not react to the robot; perception is perfect; the robot is a point with no dynamics. Robot-caused collisions on GC stay above the recorded pedestrian’s for every policy, including the oracle, because the safety filter relies on constant-velocity prediction. A learned or uncertainty-aware predictor and reactive pedestrians are future work.

Generator comparison. The flow-matching candidates were available on 900 GC scenes only (3 s horizon, people in the planner’s window), so the comparison uses that horizon, those people and four features.

6 Conclusion

We separated what a crowd-navigation robot can do – safe paths decoded from a codebook of human walking behaviours – from which of them a particular user wants, and learned the latter from a handful of A/B questions. Five EUBO queries cut the simple regret on unseen situations from that of the human-data prior (0.45–0.47) to 0.09–0.12. Choosing the questions with the posterior, by EUBO or by information gain, matters more than the estimator, and the candidate set bounds what any preference can achieve. Next, the user study of Sec. 4.6 will test whether real people differ enough that a personal θ̂\hat\theta predicts their held-out answers better than a shared one, and closed-loop tests with reactive pedestrians and a learned predictor will show what the preference changes once the crowd responds to the robot.
